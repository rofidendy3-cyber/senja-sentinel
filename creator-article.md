# No Proof, No Signature: Why Agents That Move Money Need Verifiable Checks

I let an AI agent operate two crypto wallets for me. Not a demo wallet, not a paper account — two live agentic wallets on BNB Smart Chain, with real (if modest) assets, that the agent uses every day to claim free mints, manage listings, and watch markets while I do other things.

People usually ask about the upside. I think the more interesting question is the one I had to answer before any of that was safe: *when the agent says "I checked, it's fine" — why should I believe it?*

An agent's "I checked" is just text. It is generated by the same system that might have misread the contract, mis-copied a number, or been fed a convincing lie by a stranger. Over the past months of running this setup, I have watched each of those failure modes actually happen — and the system only survived them because we stopped treating the agent's word as evidence, and started demanding proof-shaped checks instead. This article is about what those checks look like, where they still fall short, and why I think zero-knowledge proof verification is the missing layer.

## The three lies

The first lie is the **pasted instruction**. Instructions for a mint arrived one day claiming the target contract was verified and safe to call. The audit said otherwise: the contract had no verified source, the function selector was unknown, and the flow depended on signature bytes supplied by a third party. If the agent had treated pasted text as authorisation, it would have signed a call nobody could explain. Our rule now: anything pasted in is data to be verified on-chain, never a command. A general "go ahead" from me does not authorise an unverified, unknown-function call — ever.

The second lie is the **preview**. Wallet software shows you a simulation before you sign, and it is natural to treat that simulation as a promise. It is not. In one free claim, the preview displayed a credit of 16.8 million tokens; the executed transaction delivered 8.4 million — a different, lower tier. Nothing was lost because the claim was free, but the lesson generalised: previews are advisory. So our gates are written against what a preview *must structurally contain* to be safe (no risk flags, no allowance changes, no authority changes, the expected credit and nothing else) and against on-chain results after the fact — and a listing price is verified by decoding the actual hex in the calldata back into the human currency amount and requiring an exact match. If the decode doesn't match the intent, the signature doesn't happen.

The third lie is the **spot price**. A thin-pool token can show a delightful dollar value that no buyer will ever pay you. We only value a holding by an executable quote for the full amount we actually hold, and our standing sell rules require net proceeds — after marketplace fees, royalties, and gas — to be strictly positive. Several of our balances are, by that honest measure, worth zero. An agent that reports spot prices to its human is not informing them; it is flattering them.

## The checker has bugs too

The most instructive failure was our own. For a first-come-first-served free mint, we pre-armed an autonomous snipe: simulate, gate-check, execute, back to back, because human reaction time loses those races. The gate code, however, tested the wallet's risk report at the wrong level of nesting. Every clean preview therefore read as "risky", the snipe politely aborted, and the free pool was gone in under a minute — drained by bots whose gates were dumber and faster.

We fixed the gate and added a rule that any gate must be validated against a known-clean preview before it is armed. But notice what kind of failure this was: not malice, not a scam — a *checker* bug, invisible until the one moment it mattered. "Who verifies the verifier?" is not a philosophy question when an agent holds signing authority. It is an engineering ticket.

## What proofs would change

Here is the uncomfortable summary of the system above: every check produces a verdict, and the only evidence for that verdict is a log written by the same agent that acted on it. I trust the system because I built the rules and read the logs — which is to say, the trust still terminates in me, doing spot checks, forever. That does not scale to a world where agents transact with strangers' agents, or where one human supervises fifty agents.

This is where a proof-verification layer like **zkVerify** fits, and it fits precisely because it does not ask you to trust the prover. Imagine each gate emitting a small, cheaply verifiable proof instead of a log line:

- a proof that the exact calldata about to be signed decodes to the parameters a human approved — this price, this recipient, this token, nothing else;
- a proof that the contract being called has a deployed bytecode hash matching a specific verified source code;
- a proof that the pre-signing simulation contained no allowance or authority changes.

None of these proofs need to reveal the wallet's strategy, its other holdings, or its owner's identity. That is the zero-knowledge part doing real work: the *check* becomes public and verifiable while the *context* stays private. zkVerify's whole purpose — verifying proofs cheaply and at scale, across proof systems — is the infrastructure version of the rule we run by hand: an agent's claim becomes something any third party can confirm in milliseconds, without re-running the audit and without taking the agent's word for anything.

I am not claiming this exists end-to-end today for small wallet agents like mine. It doesn't. The gates described above are scripts and discipline, not circuits. But the direction is clear, and it is the same direction whether the stakes are a free NFT mint or an autonomous treasury: the future agent does not say "trust me, I checked." It hands you the proof, and you — or your agent — verify it before value moves.

Until then, our rule stands, and I would offer it to anyone giving an agent signing authority: **no proof, no signature.** Make the agent show its work in a form that can be checked by someone other than itself. The scams are already automated. The verification has to be, too.
